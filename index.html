Job market paper

Gender Hiring Quotas

with Eliana Sena

Draft available soon

Abstract

Gender quotas have mostly targeted corporate boards and done little for the gender employment gap. Requiring a minimum share of women in a firm's entire workforce would force firms to lay off workers or hire beyond their needs. A quota on new hires avoids these costs, but firms can comply and then undo its effect through separations. We study a novel type of quota: Italian firms that win public contracts must reserve for women at least 30 percent of the new hires needed for the project. Coverage depends on how contracts are funded, so similar contracts are awarded with and without the quota. Linking procurement records to employer-employee data and balance sheets, we compare winners of contracts with and without the quota in a matched difference-in-differences design. Three years after the award, female employment in firms subject to the quota is 11 percent higher than in comparable winners without it, with no difference for men, although some firms avoid the requirement by hiring less. Female separations rise once the contract ends, but do not fully offset the additional hires. The new jobs are mostly white-collar but temporary and part-time, with no effect on wages. We find no evidence of efficiency losses. Hiring quotas can bring women into male-dominated firms, but their effects partly fade once the obligation expires.

Psychosocial stress, hormones, and impulsivity/risk-taking in economic decision-making

with JohnMichael Jurgensen, Nora Nickels McLean, Javier I. Borráz-León, Luigi Guiso, Aldo Rustichini, and Dario Maestripieri

Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics, Vol. 112, 2024

Abstract

This study explored the relationships between acute psychosocial stress, hormones, probability and delay discounting, and time perspective in men and women. Study participants were 120 (60 male, 60 female) young adults. Half of them underwent the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST) and half served as controls. Saliva samples were collected from all participants and concentrations of testosterone and cortisol were measured. Risk taking and impulsivity were assessed with a probability discounting and a delay discounting task, respectively. Participants filled out several questionnaires, including the Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory (ZTPI). The TSST was associated with an increase in salivary cortisol concentrations in males and females, and with an increase in testosterone in males, but not in females. Variation in probability discounting was predicted by changes in testosterone (the higher the testosterone the greater the risk-taking) and by the ZTPI, whereas variation in delay discounting scores was not predicted by hormones, the ZTPI, or other variables. Our study contributes to research on psychosocial stress, hormones, and decision-making by reporting, for the first time, an association between stress-induced changes in testosterone and economic risk-taking. Our study also suggests that risk-taking and impulsivity are different processes associated with different psychological and physiological mechanisms.

Working papers

The Early Math Gender Gap

Single-authored

Draft available upon request

Abstract

The gender gap in mathematics (MGG) — defined as the difference between boys' and girls' standardized test scores — has profound implications for educational trajectories and labour-market outcomes. Leveraging rich administrative data from Rome's public preschool allocation system linked to nationally standardized test scores (INVALSI), we want to document the timing of the MGG's emergence and evaluate the role of early-childhood education in shaping this gap. We show that 70% of the MGG observed at the end of high-school is already present by grade 2. Exploiting quasi-random variation in preschool assignment, we want to estimate intention-to-treat effects that highlight heterogeneous impacts of preschool quality across gender. The aim of this study is to underscore the importance of interventions before compulsory schooling to mitigate gender disparities in mathematical ability.
